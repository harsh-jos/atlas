## What was exercised

A separate new chat then retrieved and revised the second run's saved explanation.
Each evaluation used its own disposable local account; the return chat reused the second account.

## Findings and changes

The first run still assumed calculus from basic algebra, over-expanded the detour, and said a
zero gradient meant a small change could not affect the output. Its follow-up also overgeneralized
that a step of 1 is too large. The prompt was revised to use numerical changes before calculus,
avoid defining a term with harder terms, keep optional analogies optional, and check claim boundaries.

In the second run, the first three answers were 248, 220, and 193 whitespace-delimited words,
compared with 316, 466, and 299 in the first. Code is included in those counts. The questions
were not identical: the second detour explicitly requested a zero-gradient counterexample.
This is directional evidence of improved scope, not a controlled comparison.

The second run:

- Started with `y = 3*x + 2`, explained a numerical change, then showed PyTorch code.
- Linked two primary documentation pages without adding a large research footer.
- Correctly explained that `y=x*x` can change away from zero despite a zero gradient there.
- Distinguished the actual increase of 7 from the local prediction of 6 at `x=3`.
- Saved a real Markdown explanation and returned a working reader URL.
- Remembered explored concepts and a prerequisite connection, without mastery labels.
- Stopped with a short acknowledgement and no new work.

The new chat found the saved explanation without the original conversation's messages. It then
read and revised the existing record, preserved its source, and added the requested zero-gradient
counterexample. The persisted body was inspected, not just the model's success message.

## Remaining quality limits

The model still offered two optional directions in places and gave a longer-than-needed revision
acknowledgement. After this review, instructions were tightened to one next question at most and
a link plus one sentence for save/revision acknowledgements. Those final wording changes were
not evaluated in another live run. It also used “linear rate of change” without fully explaining
“linear”; this remains a language-quality improvement to pursue.

The local-change explanation is much better, but some summaries still say predictions are accurate
“only” for small steps. That is too broad as a general statement. Keep checking examples against
counterexamples instead of treating fluent answers as correct.

Research and high reasoning make initial replies slow. The second five-turn run used 10 model
requests and one grounded research request; the provider reported 13 search queries inside that
request. A research-request count is not a count of Google's individual queries. No hard currency
cap or deterministic teaching/consent checker is implemented.
